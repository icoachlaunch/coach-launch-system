How to Tell a Story

Read~5 min
DoOne action

This chapter in one minute
  1. Chapter 9 covered which story to tell and why. This chapter is how you tell it out loud.
  2. Tell a scene, not a summary. One moment, one place, something that was actually said.
  3. The pause does more work than any word. Slow down where it matters and stop before the turn.
  4. Rehearse it out loud until you can tell it without notes.

01

Why it works

Think about two people telling you about the same holiday. One says, "We had a great time, the weather was lovely and the food was amazing." The other says, "On the second night we got caught in a storm, and the only place open was a tiny bar where an old man was cooking fish over a drum." You'll forget the first one by tomorrow. You can already see the second.

A summary gives people information. A scene puts them in the room, and people feel what they can picture.

That matters in a presentation because a decision is made on feeling and explained afterwards with logic. If your room only gets the summary, they agree with you and do nothing. If they're standing in the scene with you, they feel what you felt, and the point of the story lands without you having to argue it.

02

The framework: five things that make a story land

WhatHow you do it
One scenePick a single moment. A time, a place, who was there
Something that was saidOne line of real dialogue, in the words that were used
What you could see and hearTwo or three details, no more. The ones that make it real
The pauseStop before the turn. Let the room get there before you say it
The room takes partA question at the start, a word to say inside it, the soundbyte typed at the end

One scene

"I struggled for years" is a summary. "It's 11 o'clock on a Sunday night, I've got 40 slides open, and I still don't know where the offer goes" is a scene.

You don't need the whole history. You need the moment it turned. Start as close to that moment as you can.

Something that was said

A line of dialogue does what a paragraph of description can't. "Hey Matthew, I think everybody has left" is the entire story in seven words.

Keep it short. One or two lines is plenty. If you go back and forth between people more than a few times, the room loses track of who's talking.

What you could see and hear

Pick the details that only someone who was there would know: the heavy one-fin board when everyone else had three, the sound of the waves at night. Two or three is enough. More than that and the detail becomes the story.

Every detail should serve the one point you're making. If it doesn't, cut it, however much you like it.

Telling it as if it's happening now

Try telling the key moment in the present tense. "So I'm sitting there, staring at an empty room" pulls people in closer than "I was sitting there." You don't have to do the whole story this way. Use it for the moment that matters.

The pause

The most common mistake is rushing the turn. You've told the story, you can feel the room with you, and you hurry to the lesson because the silence feels long.

It feels long to you. To them it's the moment the story lands. Stop. Count two in your head. Then say the line.

Slow down as you approach the turn, drop your voice, and let the last sentence before it stand on its own.

Then the soundbyte

Say the one line. Pause. Say it again, slower. Then move on quickly. A story that's followed by two minutes of explanation has just been un-told.

03

Engaging the room

  • Before you start: the hook is a question. "How many of you have ever…? Type ME." Wait for the chat to fill.
  • Inside the story: give them a word. "Everybody say [the word]." Or leave a sentence unfinished and let them finish it.
  • At a decision point: "Good idea or bad idea? Type it."
  • At the turn: "How many of you have had a moment like that?"
  • At the end: have them type the soundbyte.

A story with the room taking part is a conversation. A story without it is a speech.

04

Right and wrong

Scene or summary

✕ Not that

"I went through a really hard time in my business."

✓ Do this

"My MC sent me a message 15 minutes in: 'Hey Matthew, I think everybody has left.'"

The turn

✕ Not that

Rushing from the story straight into the lesson without a breath.

✓ Do this

Slowing down, stopping, counting two, then saying the line.

The detail

✕ Not that

Ten details, because you remember all of them.

✓ Do this

Two or three, chosen because they make the point.

After the story

✕ Not that

"So what that story really teaches us is… and another thing it shows is…"

✓ Do this

The soundbyte, twice, and on to the next beat.

05

The steps

  1. Pick the story and name its one point in a sentence.
  2. Find the moment. The single scene where it turned.
  3. Write the line that was said.
  4. Choose two or three details you could see or hear.
  5. Mark the pause. Put a line in your notes where you stop.
  6. Write the soundbyte. One line they could repeat.
  7. Tell it out loud, standing up, three times. Then tell it once to another person.

06

Mistakes to avoid

  • Reading it. A story that's read sounds read. Know it well enough to tell it.
  • Starting too early. You don't need the three years before the moment. Start at the moment.
  • Being the hero. The room connects with the struggle, not the win. Let yourself be wobbly in it.
  • Explaining the moral. If the story needs explaining, it's the wrong story or it's told wrong.
  • Never practising out loud. It always sounds different in your head.

07

Check your understanding

  1. What's the difference between a scene and a summary? A scene is one moment with a place, a person and something that was said. A summary describes a period of time.
  2. Where does the pause go? Just before the turn, and again after the soundbyte.
  3. How many details does a story need? Two or three that serve the point.
