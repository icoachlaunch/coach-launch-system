Stories That Sell

01

Why it works

Think of a teacher you still remember from school. You've almost certainly forgotten what they taught you in any given lesson. What you remember is a story they told.

Facts ask people to agree with you. A story lets them reach the conclusion themselves, and people rarely argue with a conclusion they've reached on their own.

That matters most at the moments when your room is holding on to a belief that will stop them saying yes. "I can work this out myself." "I haven't got time." "It costs too much." Tell someone they're wrong about any of those and they'll defend it. Tell them a story about a monkey with its fist stuck in a coconut, and they'll laugh, and then they'll see themselves.

A story changes what people believe without making them wrong for having believed it. That's why it's a mechanism of both transformation and selling.

02

The framework: three kinds of story

KindWhat it isThe job it does
Your $Million Moment™The true story of the moment you discovered your Magic Formula™, told as a sceneTrust. You've stood where they're standing
Your clients' storiesReal people who've already made the tripProof. It works for people like them
Story bitsShort, repeatable stories and parables you slot inOne job at one moment: wake the room, build belief, break a belief, answer an objection, or prime the decision

Your $Million Moment™ is its own step of the bigger system. This chapter is about the story bits, because they're the ones you choose and place.

What a story bit does

A bit isn't filler. It does one job at one moment.

JobWhat it doesWhere it goes
EngagementWakes the room up and sets the two-way contractPhase 1 · WHO
Hype / empowering beliefBuilds belief that change is possible for themPhases 1 and 2 · WHO and WHAT
Belief-breakDissolves a belief that would stop them buyingPhase 3 · WHY
Objection-breakAnswers time, money, or "what will people think"Phase 4 · WHEN
Close-primeMakes the cost of standing still the biggest number in the roomJust before the price

There's no bit for the pitch itself. Your pitch is built for your offer. It isn't a story you can swap in.

The six parts of a bit

PartWhat it is
HookA question that gets hands up or answers in the chat before the story starts
StoryThe metaphor or narrative, simple and vivid, with the room taking part
TurnThe bridge from the story to them. "How many of you have found yourself…"
TargetThe belief or objection it breaks. This is why the bit is in the room
SoundbyteThe one line they'll repeat afterwards
TransitionThe hand-off into the next part

One bit, taken apart: The Crossroads

  • Hook: "How many of you have something you've been thinking about doing for way too long? Type GUILTY."
  • Story: You're standing at a crossroads. One road is the life you've got, the other is the life you want. You stand there thinking, researching, asking everyone. And while you stand there, the sun is going down.
  • Turn: "You didn't avoid a choice. You chose the road you're already on."
  • Target: "I'll decide later. I need more time to think."
  • Soundbyte: "Not deciding is a decision. It just picks the worst option for you."
  • Transition: "So today, you're a decider. Type DECIDER."

Notice it runs early, long before any offer. By the time the offer arrives, "let me think about it" already sounds like the wrong road.

Pick by target, not by taste

Don't choose the bit you like best. Choose the one whose target matches a belief your Golden Avatar™ actually holds. A brilliant story aimed at a belief the room doesn't have is three wasted minutes at the moment you can least afford them.

The one rule

Any story you tell as your own must be true and yours.

Classic parables are shared. Retell them in your own words and credit where they came from: "There's an old story about…" What you never do is pass off someone else's life as something that happened to you. A live room can feel a fake, and when they catch it they discount everything you said before it.

The same goes for numbers. If you can't verify a figure, cut it. The story carries the weight, not the statistic.

03

Engaging the room

A story is where a room is most tempted to sit back and listen. A good bit doesn't let them.

  • The hook is always a question. "How many of you have ever…? Type ME."
  • Give them a word to say inside the story. "Everybody say [the word]."
  • Let them finish your sentence. "What goes up must come…" and wait.
  • Ask them to judge. "Good idea or bad idea? Type it."
  • Have them type the soundbyte. Once they've typed it, it's theirs.

04

Right and wrong

The job

✕ Not that

A story you love that has nothing to do with what the room believes.

✓ Do this

A story chosen because its target is the exact belief standing between your room and a yes.

Whose story

✕ Not that

"This happened to a friend of mine…" when it's a parable you read.

✓ Do this

"There's an old story about how to catch a monkey…"

The ending

✕ Not that

Finishing the story and going straight to the next slide.

✓ Do this

The turn, the soundbyte said twice, and a line that hands off to what's next.

The moral

✕ Not that

Explaining the lesson for two minutes after the story.

✓ Do this

One turn, one line, and trusting them to get it.

05

The steps

  1. List the beliefs in your room. What does your Golden Avatar™ believe that would stop them saying yes?
  2. List the objections. Time, money, the people around them, having tried before.
  3. Match one story to each, by target. One job, one story.
  4. Write each one in the six parts. If you can't name the target in one sentence, the bit isn't ready.
  5. Point the turn and the soundbyte at your room. Their words, their world.
  6. Check whose story it is. True and yours, or credited.
  7. Place each bit in its phase. Engagement and belief early, belief-breaks and objections around the pitch.

06

Mistakes to avoid

  • Choosing by taste. The best story is the one that fits the belief in the room.
  • Two stories doing the same job. One money story, one time story. A second one weakens the first.
  • Inventing details or numbers. One wrong fact and the room stops trusting the rest.
  • Using a story to push. A bit names a pattern. It never pressures someone past a real no.
  • Letting it run long. A bit earns attention for what comes next. It isn't the main event.

07

Check your understanding

  1. What are the three kinds of story in a presentation? Your $Million Moment™, your clients' stories, and story bits.
  2. What are the six parts of a bit? Hook, Story, Turn, Target, Soundbyte, Transition.
  3. How do you choose which bit to tell? By its target: the belief or objection your room actually holds.
