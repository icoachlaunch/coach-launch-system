01

Why it works

Think about catching a flight. You don't plan to arrive at the gate as it closes. You work backwards from the time the doors shut and leave yourself room, because something always takes longer than you thought.

A presentation is the same, and the thing at the gate is your offer. Teaching always takes longer live than it did in your head. You tell an extra story, the chat is lively, a question comes in. Every one of those minutes comes off the end, and the end is where the pitch, the close and the re-pitch live.

That's how good presenters end up making their offer in the last four minutes to a room that's already looking at the clock. They ran out of time, and the offer was the only thing left to squeeze.

Timing is how you protect the part of the event that the whole event exists for.

02

The framework: work backwards from the close

Your event's length comes from the Immersion Timeline in Chapter 3. Whatever that length is, you plan it from the end, not from the start.

StepWhat you do
1 · Fix the endThe time your event finishes. It doesn't move
2 · Protect Phase 4The re-pitch, the questions and the send-off get their time first
3 · Protect the pitchPhase 3 gets its time next
4 · Give the rest to Phases 1 and 2Trust and the win get what's left, and it's enough
5 · Leave some emptyPlan a little less than fits. Live always runs longer

The run sheet

A run sheet is your presentation as a list of beats with a clock time beside each one. Not "ten minutes for the story" but the actual time on the clock when the story should be finished.

Put a second clock time beside the three moments that matter most: when Phase 2 must end, when the offer must start, and when the re-pitch must start. If you only watch three times during your event, watch those.

Cut depth, never the order

When you're running long, there's a right way and a wrong way to get the time back.

Cut depth: one proof point instead of three. A shorter version of a story. Two examples in a teaching section instead of four.

Never cut: a whole phase, the bridge into your pitch, the price, the step for your close, the re-pitch, or handing over your Event Magnet™.

Decide before the event what goes first if you're behind. Mark those parts in your notes. Deciding live, with a room watching, is how the wrong thing gets cut.

A longer event is the same shape

A longer event doesn't get more phases. It gets more depth in the same four: more proof, more story, more of the dream shown. On a multi-day event, the phases spread across the days.

Start on time, and start strong

Waiting for latecomers punishes the people who came on time, and the first minutes are when the room decides whether to stay. Start when you said you would, with your hook, not with housekeeping.

Let your MC keep the clock

You shouldn't be watching the time while you're telling a story. Give your MC the run sheet and agree a simple private signal for "you're five minutes behind". That's one less thing in your head.

03

Engaging the room

Engagement takes time, and it's time well spent, so plan for it.

  • Budget for the waiting. Asking for a YES and waiting for it to come in takes longer than you think. Put it in the run sheet.
  • Don't cut the questions to catch up. A silent room that finishes on time doesn't buy. Cut a proof point before you cut a response.
  • Use the chat to save time. "Type your biggest question" and answer the three that come up most, not every one.
  • Tell them where you are. "We're about to get to the part I promised you" resets attention when the room is tiring.

04

Right and wrong

✕ Not that

Dropping two proof points earlier so the re-pitch gets its full time.

An MC with the run sheet and a signal.

05

The steps

  1. Write your event's length and finish time at the top of a page.
  2. List every beat of your presentation, in order.
  3. Work backwards from the finish, giving Phase 4 and Phase 3 their time first.
  4. Write a clock time beside each beat.
  5. Mark your three checkpoints: Phase 2 ends, the offer starts, the re-pitch starts.
  6. Mark what you'll cut first if you're behind.
  7. Do one timed run-through out loud, then fix the run sheet with the real times.

06

Mistakes to avoid

  • Timing it in your head. It's always shorter in your head. Say it out loud with a clock running.
  • Filling every minute. Leave room. Something always takes longer.
  • Over-teaching to be generous. The extra teaching comes out of the offer's time, and the offer is how you actually help them.
  • Cutting the close to finish on time. If something has to give, it's depth earlier on.
  • Going over without saying so. If you'll run past the finish, tell the room and tell them why it's worth staying.

07

Check your understanding

  1. Which end of your event do you plan from? The end. The pitch and the re-pitch get their time first.
  2. What do you cut when you're running long? Depth: a proof point, part of a story, an example. Never a phase or the pitch.
  3. What are the three checkpoints on your run sheet? When Phase 2 ends, when the offer starts, and when the re-pitch starts.
