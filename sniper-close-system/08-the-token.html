01

Why it works

Imagine two people trying to sell you the same holiday. The first describes it: the flights, the hotel, the number of nights. The second slides a boarding pass across the table with your name on it.

It's the same holiday. But the second one feels real, because there's something in front of you that you can look at and almost pick up.

A high-ticket offer is hard to picture. It's months of work, calls, support and change, and none of that fits on a slide. So the room tries to weigh up something they can't see, and when people can't see what they're buying, they wait.

The Token solves that. It gives the whole offer one visible face. When they can see it and they want it, the decision gets simpler: it stops being "do I want a program?" and becomes "do I want that?"

02

The framework: what makes a Token

A Token has three things, and it needs all of them.

VisualThey can see it on the screen or in your handsThe room can't want what it can't picture
Part of your offerIt belongs to what you're selling, and isn't a random bonusWanting the Token leads straight to wanting the offer
High in perceived valueIt looks and feels like the thing that holds the answersIt carries the worth of the whole offer in one object

The Sniper Close Sequence puts it in one line: the Token turns your offer into something they can see.

What a Token can look like

A secret roadmap. A black book. A workbook. One thing, with a name, that you can hold up.

Where it sits in your presentation

Phase 2 is where the room gets the Token. You teach one piece of your Magic Formula™ through your Event Magnet™, and by the end of the phase they can see the dream on the other side of their pain and they want what you're holding.

Your Event Magnet™ is a critical piece of your presentation, so this training shows you what one looks like and how it works in the room. The builder that makes yours is part of the full system.

03

Engaging the room

The Token is the easiest thing in your presentation to get a response on, because it's a thing and people react to things.

  • When you first hold it up: "Who wants this? Give me a YES in the chat."
  • When you name it: "Type [its name] in the chat." They've now said the name themselves.
  • After you show what it does: "How many of you here could have used this last month?"
  • Before the offer: "Type MINE if you want this in your hands."

04

Right and wrong

Describing and showing

✕ Not that

"You'll get twelve modules, weekly calls and a community."

✓ Do this

Holding up one named thing: "This is [your Token]. Everything we do together is in here."

A list makes them do sums. A thing makes them want.

Part of the offer

✕ Not that

A gift card or a gadget thrown in as a bonus.

✓ Do this

Something that only makes sense inside your offer, so wanting it means wanting the offer.

One, and only one

✕ Not that

Five things held up one after another.

✓ Do this

One thing, with one name, shown more than once.

05

The steps

  1. Choose the one thing. What single object could stand for your whole offer?
  2. Name it. A name they can type and repeat.
  3. Make it visible. If it's a document, show the real thing. If you're on camera, hold it up.
  4. Write one line on what it does. Plain words, one result.
  5. Mark where you show it. In Phase 2 when they get it, and again when you make the offer.
  6. Write the question you'll ask each time. No showing without a response from the room.

06

Mistakes to avoid

  • Describing it and never showing it. If they can't see it, it isn't a Token.
  • A Token that has nothing to do with the offer. It gets a reaction and sells nothing.
  • More than one. The power is in one thing they remember.
  • Talking it down. "It's just a little worksheet" tells the room what it's worth. Treat it like it holds the answers, because it does.
  • Showing it once. They need to see it more than once to want it.

07

Check your understanding
