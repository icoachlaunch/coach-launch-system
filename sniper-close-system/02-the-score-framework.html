The SCORE™ Framework

This chapter in one minute
  1. The SCORE™ Framework is the foundation the whole Sniper Close™ System is built on.
  2. It's five acts, like the acts of a play: Serendipity, Connection, Opportunity, Reality and Execution.
  3. The order never changes, because it's the order people actually decide in.
  4. The four phases of your presentation are how those five acts get performed in a live room.

01

Why it works

Think about the last really good play or film you saw. It didn't open with the ending. First you were pulled in, then you came to care about someone, then you saw what was possible for them, then everything was put at stake, and only then did it resolve. If you'd been shown those scenes in a different order, you'd have walked out.

A decision works the same way. Nobody decides in one jump. They notice, then they trust, then they believe, then they weigh it up, and then they act. That order isn't a sales technique. It's how people make up their minds about anything that matters.

SCORE™ is that order, written down as five acts. Each act earns the thing the next one needs. Attention makes trust possible. Trust makes the answer believable. Belief gives the stakes their weight. And once the stakes are real, the next step feels like the natural end of the story.

That's why a presentation built on SCORE™ doesn't feel like being sold to. The room feels like they discovered something.

02

The framework: five acts

ActNameThe room thinks…Its jobWhat it earns
1 · SSerendipity"This is about me."Stop them with relevance, not hypeAttention
2 · CConnection"This person gets me."Show you understand them from the insideTrust
3 · OOpportunity"So that's the answer."Reveal the way from their pain to the outcomeBelief
4 · RReality"This is true for me, and staying put costs more."Hold up the truth of both futures, and clear the last belief in the wayUrgency
5 · EExecution"The next step is obvious."Make one clear action feel easy and naturalThe decision

The order never changes

Run an act early and it lands on a room that isn't ready for it. Show them the answer before they trust you, and it sounds like a pitch. Ask for the decision before the stakes are real, and it feels like pressure.

Every act can be longer or shorter. A short event gives each act a few minutes. A multi-day event gives each one hours. The order stays exactly the same.

One big idea

A play is about one thing. So is your presentation. Serendipity opens a loop around one idea, every act moves that idea forward, and Execution closes it.

Five strong acts about five different things are five separate talks. Before you write a word, say your big idea in one sentence. If it takes two sentences, your presentation is about two things.

The hand-off

Each act ends by creating the need for the next one.

  • Serendipity leaves a question that Connection answers.
  • Connection leaves a pain that Opportunity resolves.
  • Opportunity leaves a doubt, "but would it work for me?", that Reality settles.
  • Reality leaves a choice that Execution asks them to make.

The joins between the acts are where a room drifts away. A good hand-off carries them across.

The five acts inside the four phases

The four phases you met in Chapter 1 are the five acts, staged for a live room.

PhaseThe acts it performsWhat the room goes through
Phase 1 · WHOAct 1 · Serendipity, then Act 2 · Connection"This is about me" and "this person gets me"
Phase 2 · WHATAct 3 · Opportunity"So that's the answer", through one piece and one real win
Phase 3 · WHYAct 4 · Reality, then Act 5 · Execution begins"It's true for me, and staying put costs more." Then the offer
Phase 4 · WHENAct 5 · Execution, carried through"The next step is obvious", for everyone who needed one more pass

Two things follow from that table, and they'll save you a lot of trouble.

Trust takes two acts, so Phase 1 holds two. Getting their attention and earning their trust are different jobs. A room can notice you without believing you understand them. Phase 1 does both before you teach anything.

What's stopping them comes in two kinds. The beliefs inside their own head ("this won't work for someone like me") belong to Reality, in Phase 3. The practical objections ("I haven't got the time", "it costs too much") belong to Execution, in Phase 4. Answer them in the wrong act and they don't land.

Where SCORE™ goes after this

SCORE™ isn't only for presentations. It's the shape of every piece of copy and content in the bigger system: an ad, an email, a page, a video. What changes from one to the next is how deep each act goes, never the order.

Your own material, arranged into the five acts, is called your SCORE™ Card. It's one of the steps of the full system.

03

Engaging the room

Each act asks the room for a different kind of response.

  • Serendipity: a question nearly everyone says yes to. "How many of you here have…?"
  • Connection: recognition. "Type ME if you've had a moment like that."
  • Opportunity: a response to the win. "Type what you've just written down."
  • Reality: a choice. "Type WHOLE if you're someone who runs the whole thing."
  • Execution: an action. "Type DONE when you've done it."

If you notice the room's answers getting quieter, look at which act you're in. It usually means the act before didn't earn what this one needs.

04

Right and wrong

The order

✕ Not that

Opening with your method, because it's the part you're proudest of.

✓ Do this

Opening with them. Your method arrives in Act 3, once they trust you.

The big idea

✕ Not that

A presentation that covers everything you know.

✓ Do this

One idea, opened in Act 1, carried through every act, and closed in Act 5.

Beliefs and objections

✕ Not that

Answering "I can't afford it" before they believe it would even work for them.

✓ Do this

Beliefs first, in Reality. Practical objections after, in Execution.

The joins

✕ Not that

"Right, moving on to the next section."

✓ Do this

Ending each act on the question the next one answers.

05

The steps

  1. Write your big idea in one sentence. If it takes two, choose one.
  2. Write the five acts down the side of a page.
  3. Beside each act, write the one thing the room should be thinking by the end of it, in their words.
  4. Write the hand-off between each pair of acts. One line that makes them need the next act.
  5. Mark which phase each act sits in, using the table above.
  6. Check the order. Nothing that belongs to a later act has crept into an earlier one.

06

Mistakes to avoid

  • Skipping Serendipity and Connection. Going straight to your method is the most common mistake, and it's why good content gets a thank-you and no sale.
  • Treating the acts as boxes to tick. They're a journey. Each one has to earn the next.
  • More than one big idea. The room can follow one story. Give them two and they keep neither.
  • Rushing Reality. It's the act that turns "that's interesting" into "I need this". Without it, the offer arrives with nothing at stake.
  • Asking early. A call to action before Reality has done its work feels like pressure, however gently you say it.

07

Check your understanding

  1. What are the five acts, in order? Serendipity, Connection, Opportunity, Reality, Execution.
  2. Why does the order never change? Because each act earns what the next one needs: attention, then trust, then belief, then urgency, then the decision.
  3. Which acts does Phase 1 perform? Two: Serendipity and Connection. Attention first, then trust.
