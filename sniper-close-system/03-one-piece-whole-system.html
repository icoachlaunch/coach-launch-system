01

The first wave

Matthew White, in his own words.

When I moved down to the coast, we lived in an apartment right on the beachfront, and I used to fall asleep at night listening to the waves crashing. In the mornings I'd walk down to the main beach and watch the surfers ride one wave after the next… and all I wanted was to be out there with them.

One day my dad bought me a surfboard. We didn't have much money, so it was the cheap one, bigger and heavier than it should have been. It had one fin, and all the cool surfers had three.

I went out and gave it a crack anyway, and I got dumped, and dumped, and dumped again. The guys used to laugh at me and tell me I was useless. But one of them had started showing me how to snap up onto the board and drop into the wave. And one afternoon the waves were just right, and I heard him shouting, "Go, go, go! Paddle, paddle, paddle!" So I paddled with everything I had, and all of a sudden I snapped up, I dropped in, and I was STANDING.

It wasn't perfect. I was wobbly, I was nervous, and I didn't stay on that board for long.

And here's the part that matters for your presentation. That wave didn't satisfy me… it did the opposite. I paddled straight back out for another one, and I kept paddling back out for years.

Nobody catches their first wave and goes home… they paddle straight back out.

02

Why it works

The one piece you teach in your presentation is the room's first wave. You're not trying to hand them the whole ocean in one event. You're trying to get them standing up once.

Three things happen when they do.

1. They believe in themselves, not just in you. Watching you succeed proves your Magic Formula™ works. Doing one piece of it themselves proves they can do it. A high-ticket yes needs both.

2. They can now see what's missing. Nobody is curious about nine steps they know nothing about. Once they're holding one piece, the pieces around it become a gap they can feel.

3. They've started, and people finish what they've started. Before the win they were thinking about beginning. After it, they're already one step in, and walking away now means leaving something unfinished.

Research behind these three points, for anyone who wants it: mastery experience as the strongest source of self-belief (Bandura); the information-gap account of curiosity (Loewenstein, 1994); the head-start effect on finishing (Nunes and Drèze, 2006) and the general pull to resume what's been started (Ovsiankina; Ghibellini and Meier, 2025).

How to pick your first wave

Your piece should pass these six checks.

  1. It's on your Magic Formula™. One real step of it, not a side topic.
  2. It's visual. Something they can see and hold up: a one-page tool, a map, a worksheet.
  3. It's a tool, not a lecture. They use it in the room.
  4. It gives a quick win. Something real, finished in one sitting.
  5. It's incomplete by design. It solves one problem and leaves the next one in plain sight.
  6. It leads to one next step. The whole system.

The line between a win and a giveaway

The win comes from using your Event Magnet™ in the room. Everything else in your presentation shows what your Magic Formula™ does and why it matters, never how to do it. If someone could leave your room and run your whole Magic Formula™ from what you showed them, you've given away the thing you're about to offer.

What happens straight after the win

Four beats open your pitch. Chapter 11 covers each one in full.

  1. Broken Models. Two or three things they've already tried, and why each one fails by design.
  2. The bridge. What your Event Magnet™ deliberately doesn't solve, and the question it leaves them holding. "That's one step of nine. Here are the other eight."
  3. The System Reveal. Your full Magic Formula™, with the step they've just done marked as done.
  4. Two Identities. The people who run the whole Magic Formula™, and the people who stop at one tool. The room picks.

04

Right and wrong

Wrong: the whole ocean. "Today I'm going to walk you through all nine steps of my method." By the end the room is full, grateful and finished. They've got notes on everything and a reason to buy nothing.

Right: the first wave. "Today you're going to build one thing with me, and you'll leave with it finished." They build it, it works, and they look up wanting to know what comes next.

Wrong: a piece with no win in it. A step explained on slides, with nothing for them to do. They understood it. They didn't stand up on the board.

Right: a win they did themselves. Small and imperfect is fine. Wobbly and short still counted.

05

Mistakes to avoid

  • Teaching everything to prove you're worth it. Your value isn't in how much you can fit in. It's in the one thing they can now do.
  • Worrying that a real win removes the reason to buy. It's the other way around. The win is what creates the wanting.
  • Showing the whole Magic Formula™ too early. Before the win it's a diagram. After the win it's a map with "you are here" on it.
  • Picking the hardest step. Choose the piece that gets them standing fastest.

06

Check your understanding

  1. Why does your presentation teach one piece and not your whole Magic Formula™? Because one real win creates the desire for the rest, and the rest is your offer.
  2. Where does your full Magic Formula™ first appear? At the opening of the pitch, after the win, with their piece marked on it.
  3. Where does the win come from? From using your Event Magnet™ in the room, not from being taught how to run everything.
