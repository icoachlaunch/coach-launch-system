Engagement

Read~5 min
DoOne action
Part2 · The Mechanisms
ThenOpen Loops
This chapter in one minute
  1. Engagement is one of the most important factors in a good event. It runs through every beat of every phase.
  2. It means always asking the room for a response: a YES, a word in the chat, a show of hands.
  3. A room that answers you is practising saying yes long before you make the offer.
  4. You train the room to respond in the first few minutes, then you never stop asking.

01

Why it works

Think about the difference between sitting in a lecture and sitting at a dinner table. In the lecture you can drift off, check your phone and nobody knows. At the table someone keeps turning to you and asking what you think, so you stay in the conversation.

A virtual room drifts even faster than a lecture hall, because the email, the phone and the kettle are all within arm's reach. The only thing that keeps people at the table is being asked, again and again, to take part.

Engagement does three jobs at once.

1. It keeps them awake. Drifted people don't buy. Someone who's typing in the chat is someone who's still in the room.

2. It gets them agreeing out loud. Every YES in the chat is a small agreement. By the time you reach the offer, they've said yes to you dozens of times, and one more feels natural.

3. It tells you where the room is. A flood of answers means you can move on. Silence means slow down. You can't see their faces the way you can in a physical room, so the chat is how you read them.

02

The framework: three kinds of question

KindWhat it sounds likeWhen to use it
The universal question"How many of you here have [something nearly everyone in the room has lived]?"To open a beat, and to make them feel they're among people like them
The YES"Give me a YES in the chat if that's you."After a pain, a point or a picture you want them to agree with
The word"Type [one word] in the chat if you agree."When you want a specific idea to stick, because they've typed it themselves

What makes a universal question work

It has to be something almost everyone in your room will say yes to. "How many of you here have ever lost a sale you were sure you had?" works for a room of people who sell. A question only a few people can answer leaves most of the room sitting on their hands, which teaches them not to respond.

Train the room first

Early in Phase 1, tell them plainly how today works: you'll be asking questions all the way through, and the people who answer get the most out of it. Then ask an easy one straight away and wait for the answers to come in.

That's the contract. Set it in the first few minutes and the room will keep answering for the rest of the event.

Then never stop asking

Every beat of your presentation should carry at least one question for the room. The phase chapters in Part 3 each have an "Engaging the room" section that shows you where they go.

A simple rule to start with: never go more than a few minutes without asking the room for something. If you look up and realise you've been talking for a long stretch with a still chat, stop and ask.

03

Right and wrong

The quiet room

✕ Not that

Slide after slide, no questions, a chat that hasn't moved in ten minutes.

✓ Do this

"How many of you here have [their pain]? Give me a YES." Then waiting for it.

The question nobody answers

✕ Not that

"Any questions so far?"

✓ Do this

"Type YES if that makes sense, or type HELP if you want me to go over it again."

The first one is easy to ignore. The second gives them something specific to type either way.

Asking and not waiting

✕ Not that

Asking for a YES and carrying on before anyone's had time to type.

✓ Do this

Asking, pausing, reading out a few names, and thanking them.

When the room goes quiet

✕ Not that

Ploughing on to the next slide.

✓ Do this

Slowing down, saying their struggle back to them in their words, and asking again.

04

The steps

  1. Write five universal questions for your room. Things nearly everyone you serve has lived through.
  2. Choose your words. Two or three single words you'll ask them to type at the moments that matter most.
  3. Write how you'll set the contract. One or two sentences, said in the first few minutes.
  4. Go through your presentation beat by beat and mark where each question goes. No beat without one.
  5. Decide what you'll do with the answers. Read names out, thank people, and respond to what they've typed.

Your MC makes all of this easier. They type the first YES so the room has someone to follow, they answer the simple questions in the chat so you don't have to stop, and they pass you the ones worth answering out loud. Chapter 18 covers how the two of you run the room together.

05

Mistakes to avoid

  • Saving engagement for the end. If they haven't answered you all event, they won't start when you make the offer.
  • Asking questions only a few can answer. It trains the rest of the room to stay silent.
  • Not waiting. The pause feels long to you and short to them. Give them time to type.
  • Ignoring the answers. If responding gets no reaction from you, they stop responding.
  • Treating silence as agreement. Silence is a room that's drifting. Go back and bring them in.

06

Check your understanding

  1. Why does engagement matter for the close? A room that's been answering you all event has been practising yes, and it's still awake when the offer comes.
  2. What are the three kinds of question? The universal question, the YES, and the word.
  3. What do you do when you ask and the room is silent? Slow down, say their struggle back in their words, and ask again.
