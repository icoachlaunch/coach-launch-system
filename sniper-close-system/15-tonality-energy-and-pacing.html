Tonality, Energy and Pacing

This chapter in one minute
  1. The same words land completely differently depending on how you say them.
  2. You have four dials: pace, volume, pitch and the pause.
  3. What keeps a room is change. Any one setting held for too long becomes background noise.
  4. Each phase has its own energy, and the price is said slowly and plainly.

01

Why it works

Think about a friend telling you something that matters to them. They speed up when they're excited, they slow right down for the important part, they drop their voice for the bit that's just for you. You never notice them doing it. You just can't stop listening.

Now think about someone reading a statement. Same speed, same volume, same tone from start to finish. Within a minute you've stopped hearing the words.

On a virtual event you've lost most of what a speaker in a physical room has to work with. They can't feel you walk towards them or see the whole of you. They've got a small picture of your face and the sound of your voice. So your voice is carrying far more of the presentation than it would in a room, and a flat voice loses a virtual room faster than anything else.

The good news is that this isn't a talent. It's four dials, and you already use all of them when you talk to a friend.

02

The framework: four dials

DialTurn it up to…Turn it down to…
PaceCarry energy, tell the light part of a story, build towards somethingMark what matters. The slower you say it, the more important it sounds
VolumeLift the room, celebrate, call for a responsePull them in. A quieter voice makes people lean towards the screen
PitchShow excitement and possibilityShow certainty. The price and the close sit at the bottom of your voice
The pause(There's no "up".) Use it before the thing that matters, and after it

Change is what keeps them

There's no perfect speed and no perfect volume. What holds a room is the change from one to the other. Fast into slow. Loud into quiet. Talking into silence.

If you notice you've been at one setting for a few minutes, change one dial. It doesn't matter much which.

The energy of each phase

PhaseThe energyWhat it sounds like
Phase 1 · WHOWarm and bright, then slow and close for your storyLike greeting people you're glad to see, then telling one of them something true
Phase 2 · WHATClear and steady, lifting at each winLike showing someone how to do something you love doing
Phase 3 · WHYCertainSlower, lower, no rush. This is where a rushed voice costs you most
Phase 4 · WHENCalm and patientLike someone with all the time in the world to answer a question

Certainty at the offer

The offer and the price are where most people's voices give them away. They speed up, the pitch goes up at the end of the sentence so the price sounds like a question, and they fill the silence afterwards with a nervous extra sentence.

Do the opposite. Slow down. Say the price plainly, with your voice going down at the end. Then stop talking. The silence after the price belongs to the room, not to you.

Talk to one person

A virtual room isn't a crowd. It's a lot of individuals, each alone with their screen. Talk the way you would to one person across a table, and look into the camera lens when you want them to feel you're looking at them.

Energy starts before you go live

Your energy on screen comes out lower than it feels to you, so you need a little more than feels natural. Stand up if you can. Move before you start. Have water within reach. The first two minutes set the level for everything after.

03

Engaging the room

How you ask for a response matters as much as what you ask.

  • Ask, then stop. A question followed straight away by more talking tells the room you didn't mean it.
  • Lift your energy on the ask, then go quiet and warm when you read the answers out.
  • Use the room's energy to set yours. A flood of answers means you can pick up the pace. A slow chat means slow down and bring them back in.
  • Say names. A name read out loud, warmly, changes that person's whole event.

04

Right and wrong

The price

✕ Not that

Fast, rising at the end, followed by "…which I know sounds like a lot, but…"

✓ Do this

Slow, plain, voice going down. Then silence.

The important line

✕ Not that

Said at the same speed as everything around it.

✓ Do this

A pause before it, said slowly, a pause after it.

The whole event

✕ Not that

High energy from the first minute to the last, with no change.

✓ Do this

Energy that rises and falls, so the high points feel high.

Reading

✕ Not that

Eyes moving along a script, voice flat.

✓ Do this

Eyes on the lens, talking to one person.

05

The steps

  1. Record five minutes of yourself presenting and listen back with your eyes closed.
  2. Mark the three most important lines in your presentation. Put a pause before and after each.
  3. Mark where you'll speed up. The light parts, the lists, the lead-in to something big.
  4. Practise the price out loud until you can say it slowly, with your voice going down, and then say nothing.
  5. Choose your warm-up. What you'll do in the ten minutes before you go live.
  6. Put a note where you can see it: "Slower. Change a dial."

06

Mistakes to avoid

  • One speed. Fast all the way through is as flat as slow all the way through.
  • Filling every silence. Silence is a tool. "Um" and "so" are what you say when you're scared of it.
  • Rushing the offer. It tells the room you're not sure it's worth it.
  • Shouting for energy. Energy comes from variety and from meaning it, not from volume.
  • Performing. They don't want a presenter. They want you, turned up a little.

07

Check your understanding

  1. What are the four dials? Pace, volume, pitch and the pause.
  2. What keeps a room listening? Change. Any one setting held too long becomes background noise.
  3. How do you say the price? Slowly and plainly, with your voice going down, and then you stop talking.
