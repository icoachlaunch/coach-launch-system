01

The Math That Makes It Pay

Your Money Model is the math that turns your system into a business.

It's the decision layer under your offer: which path you're on, what you charge, how the cash comes in, and how people enroll.

It matters because a great message and a great system can still go broke.

Price it too low and you bleed. Sell it the wrong way and you burn out on a launch treadmill. Skip the cash-flow math and your ad money runs out before a client ever pays you.

Get the model right and the opposite happens. The price fits the promise, the events pay for themselves, and every dollar you put in comes back with friends.

1.0x

The gate. If your model doesn't return at least what you spent to get a client within the first 7 days, at the realistic scenario, it isn't done yet.

Matthew White built Coach Launch on models that pay from week one.

$24.6 Million in high-ticket sales generated and $5.2 Million in recurring client results delivered, all sitting on math that worked before the ads ever ran.

What it is not

It's not a guess about price. "I think I can charge $5K" is a wish, not a model.

It's not your offer document. That's the Red Diamond Offer™, which comes next and describes this model in writing.

It's not fixed forever. Your model grows as you do, phase by phase. And it's not the same for everyone: the right model depends on where you are today.

The principle

If you can't show how one dollar of ad spend comes back as more than a dollar, you don't have a model yet. You have a hope.

04

Why Most Money Models Fail

Before what works, here's what doesn't: three ways coaches price and sell themselves into a hole.

1

Too cheap to surviveThe $27 workshop, the $97 mini-course

It feels safe because the price is low and the "yes" is easy. But if it costs real money to get a lead and only a few percent buy something cheap, you lose money on every dollar you spend.

And cheap buyers are the least committed. They don't show up, don't finish, and don't become the results that grow your business.

The trap: thinking a low price is a soft start. It's a slow bleed.

2

Stuck in the dead zoneThe $500–$3,000 course or program

Too expensive to buy on impulse, too cheap to justify a real sales conversation. So you "launch": a big push, a spike of sales, and then month two resets to zero.

That's not a business. It's a treadmill you sprint on forever, and it's why so many talented coaches burn out.

The trap: mistaking a good launch for a working model. The spike hides the reset.

3

High ticket, broken machineThe $5K–$10K offer, sold the old way

Free calls, no-shows, tire-kickers, and a "float" where your ad money goes out for weeks before a dollar comes back.

The price is right. The machine around it is exhausting and short of cash.

The trap: quitting a good offer because you ran out of money and nerve before payday arrived.

The real lesson

It's never just the price. It's the price, plus how the cash comes in, plus how people enroll. That trio is your money model, and the Coach Launch model fixes all three at once.

Moving part 1The event does the selling

06

Pricing Your Offer

Price isn't a feeling. It's math wrapped around a promise. Inside your phase band, two checks decide where you land.

✕ Not that

"I'll charge $497 because that feels safe," with no link to the outcome.

✓ Do this

Price to the promise and the band. A bigger outcome carries a bigger price.

Does the price match the promise? If your $Million Promise™ is life-changing, don't price it like a weekend workshop.

Can you defend it out loud? If you can't say the number without flinching, that's usually a proof problem, not a price problem. Start lower in the band and raise it as your proof grows.

Built your Products & Pricing? The Builder reads your three levels and asks which one is your core offer for this phase. It never picks for you.

If your offer is a membership, you need two prices

A membership is a high-ticket offer paid over time, not a low-ticket subscription. So you price it twice and always show both.

Monthly is the lower barrier. It's how most people join. Annual is twelve months less a saving, with 25% as the standard: at $500 a month, that's $4,500 for the year instead of $6,000.

The annual option isn't optional. It front-loads a year of cash into week one and removes the month-two decision completely.

So your math runs on the blended figureWhat an average new member pays you on day one, counting both the monthly and the annual buyers., never the monthly price alone. Monthly-only math makes a healthy membership look like it fails the gate.

Two numbers only you can supply: what share take the annual, and your monthly churn. If it's early days, model a range and replace it with real data as soon as you have it.

The Math, and the Gate

Every projection shows the formula, not just the answer. And every one runs in three scenarios: Low (a cold start), Realistic (the most likely case) and High (what's possible as you sharpen). Realistic is the one that counts.

The profitability gate

At the realistic scenario, your Day-7 returnThe cash that comes in within seven days (deposits, first payments, and ticket and VIP money) divided by what you spent on ads. has to be at least 1.0x.

In plain words: within the first seven days, the cash coming in has to at least equal what you spent to get those clients. If it doesn't, the Builder stops you.

When the gate fails, you've got three honest fixes. Raise the price inside your band, deepen the event to earn a higher price and close, or lower the ad spend and lean on organic. You choose, then you rerun the math.

Why Day 7, not Day 90

The ad bills come due in week one, long before the "eventually" money shows up. A model that pays fast keeps running.

The five stages every phase runs through

Your model is only as honest as the conversion numbers you plug into it. Every phase moves people through the same five stages. What changes is how well each stage converts.

  1. Ad spend or traffic. Divided by your cost per lead: $18 low, $15 realistic, $12 high as starting points.
  2. Registrants. Multiplied by your show rate.
  3. Attendees. The people in the room.
  4. Sessions booked or offers taken. Multiplied by your close rate.
  5. Paying clients.

These percentages get your first projection moving. They are not guarantees. Treat every one as a target to test, and replace it with your own number the moment you have it.

Two things stand out. In Phase 1, a bribe (a real reason to turn up) roughly doubles your show rate. And from Phase 2 on, the VIPs are the money: they show at 80–90%, and they're the ones who buy. Judge an event by how many VIPs are in the room, not the size of the free crowd.

08

Cash-In and the Close

The last part of your model is the mechanics: how cash comes in, and how the client actually says yes.

Cash-in

The ticket, the VIP upgrade and any order bumps for your phase. This money is what lets the event self-liquidateWhen the money the room pays you to be there covers the ad spend that filled it. your ad spend, so your leads are buyers, not browsers.

Add up everything the room pays you, divide it by your ad spend, and you have your self-liquidation ratio. Hit 1.0x and the event has bought its own traffic. Under 1.0x, the gap is what you fund yourself until the offer converts.

That's survivable, and most people start there. The point is to know the number. And a free room can't self-liquidate, which is why paid events matter from Phase 2 on.

The close: three directions

There isn't one way to close. There are three, and you pick the one that fits your offer and your phase.

CloseWhat it isThe conversationWhat it closes
The Strategy CloseThey book a session (a PASS™) and you close thereBefore the sale. The session does the sellingOne Day Offer ($997) up to high ticket ($3K–$10K)
The Membership CloseThey join your membership from the roomAfter the sale. An onboarding call gets them startedYour membership, paid monthly or annually ($5K–$15K/yr)

The onboarding call on the Membership Close isn't a sales call. The sale already happened. Its job is getting them started, because recurring revenue dies from month-two churn.

The Strategy Close deepens as you go: free while you're proving the offer, then paid as your pitch sharpens, because a paid session filters for serious buyers.

Each phase has a natural default, shown in the phase table. Treat it as a pricing guide, not a rule. What never changes is what each close is: Strategy talks before the sale, Membership talks after it, Deposit has no conversation at all.

The whole sequence

Traffic → Event (ticket / VIP) → the win from your Magic Formula™ → the offer → your close → paid client

Figure 08 · Start to finish. The more someone pays to get in front of you, the more serious they are, the higher they show and the more they buy.

The two numbers that tell you if it scales

CAC is what one client costs you: total ad spend divided by clients won. LTV is what one client is worth: the price, or for a membership, the blended first payment plus what they pay before they leave.

Put them together as LTV:CAC. Roughly 3:1 or better means you can scale on paid traffic with room for a bad month. Under 2:1, the margin is too thin. Treat 3:1 as a starting benchmark, and replace it with your own numbers.

The Math In Action

Here's a Phase 2 coach running a single-day event, with a free ticket, a $47 VIP and the Strategy Close into a $3,000 offer. Ad spend: $3,000.

The inputs
  • Phase: 2 · Execute
  • Event: single-day, free ticket + $47 VIP
  • Offer: $3,000
  • Ad spend: $3,000
The math, shown
  • Leads: $3,000 ÷ $15 CPL = 200 registrants
  • Room: 200 × 40% show rate = 80 attendees
  • Cash-in: 20 take the $47 VIP = $940 back before the offer is made
  • Net traffic cost: $3,000 − $940 = $2,060
  • Clients: even 2 enrolling at $3,000 = $6,000

$6,000 in on $3,000 spent. A model that pays from week one.

Check Your Work

Almost every broken money model fails in one of these six ways. Check yours against each one.

✕ Pricing on feelings

A number that feels safe, with no link to the outcome. It reads as "this probably won't work."

✓ Price to the promise and the band

Can't defend it yet? Start lower in the band and raise it as your proof grows.

✕ Selling above your phase

A $10K offer pitched off a 30-minute freebie with no track record.

✓ Match offer, event and phase

Earn your way up as your proof and your event depth grow.

✕ Ignoring the cash-in

Free everything, so all of the ad spend is a naked cost.

✓ Add the phase's ticket or VIP

Even a small cash-in shrinks your real cost of traffic and filters for buyers.

✕ Judging on the best case

"At a 10% conversion this makes a fortune!"

✓ Judge on realistic

If it pays in the middle scenario, the good weeks are a bonus.

✕ Forgetting the float

A model that "works over 90 days" but bleeds cash for six weeks.

✓ Clear the Day-7 gate

A model that pays fast survives. One that pays slowly runs out of runway.

✕ Benchmarks as guarantees

A plan built on "$15 leads and 40% show" as if they're promised.

✓ Replace them with your own

Use the ranges to start, then swap in your measured numbers fast.
