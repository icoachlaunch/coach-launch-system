01

The Ground Your Brand Stands On

Your Business Brand Profile is the identity layer your whole business stands on, written down in one place.

It's a short, clear document that answers the questions everything else depends on. Why does this business exist? What do you stand for? Where are you headed, and where in the world can you actually serve people?

It holds seven things: your mission and purpose, your core values, your goals, your location and service area, your positioning, your personality, and your brand promise.

Notice what's not on that list. It doesn't ask what you sell, what you charge, who your avatar is or who your competitors are.

That's deliberate. Those are real questions, but they belong to other tools. Answering them four times across four documents is how people give up somewhere in the third one.

1

One source of truth. Your Visual Style Guide, your Brand Voice and your $Million Story™ all pull from this one document. Build it once, well, and everything above it lines up.

When the foundation is clear, everything above it gets easier and more consistent. When it's fuzzy, you feel it in ads that don't sound like you, posts that wander, and offers that don't fit the person you serve.

Matthew White built Coach Launch on a clear foundation before a single ad ran: $24.6 Million in high-ticket sales generated and $5.2 Million in recurring client results delivered, all standing on a business that knew what it believed and why it existed.

What it is not

It's not your $Million Promise™. That's the sharp marketing hook you build in Step 1. Your Profile holds your brand promise: the standard every client can count on. One is what you say first. The other is who you are.

It's not your Visual Style Guide or your Brand Voice. Those are the next two Foundations, and both grow out of this one.

It's not where your offer, pricing, market or competitors live. Those go in Products & Pricing and Competitive Landscape.

And it's not a fantasy version of your business. It's the true one, said plainly, because everything downstream inherits whatever you write here.

The principle

This one stays high. It's the overarching truth about the business, not the operating detail.

03

How You'll Build It

You don't write a brand profile by staring at a blank page. You answer plain questions, one at a time, and the Builder puts your answers into one clean document.

It's seven elements and thirteen questions, plus two quick bits of context up front: roughly what your business is, and whether it's just you or there are partners.

That second one matters more than it sounds. It decides whether the whole document says "I" or "we", and your Brand Voice is built straight out of it.

Every question comes with two things attached: why it's being asked, and an example so you can see the shape of a good answer.

What you believe + where you're going + how you show up = the ground everything else stands on.

Figure 03 · The shape of a Business Brand Profile. Seven elements, one document.

Each Foundation owns its own questions

This is why the Profile won't ask about your offer or your market. Five documents only work if each one owns a question and the others leave it alone.

The moment two of them ask the same thing, you do the work twice, and you end up with two slightly different answers. So if the Builder declines to capture something, it'll tell you which document it belongs in. That's the brand altitudeStaying at the level of who you are and what you believe. Offers, prices, avatars and competitors sit lower down, in other documents. rule doing its job.

"I don't know yet" is a real answer

Some of these you'll answer in a sentence. One or two might make you realise you've never actually decided.

That's normal, and it's not a reason to invent something. A made-up answer is worse than a blank one. Say what's true, mark what isn't settled yet, and come back to it once the tool that decides it has been built.

04

The Seven, In Full

Here's each element: what it is, why it matters, how to find yours, and the trap that quietly weakens it.

1

Mission & PurposeWhy this exists

Your mission is the reason the business exists beyond making money: the change you're trying to make. Your purpose is the other half: why it's you chasing it.

Why it matters. Mission keeps you consistent when things get hard and makes the right people trust you. Purpose is the part a competitor can't copy. They can take your method. They can't take your reason.

How to find yours

For the mission, name the specific change you care about, for your specific people. What would still matter to you even if the money was handled?

For the purpose, go back to your own story. One honest sentence beats a paragraph of backstory.

✕ Not that

"To empower people to become the best version of themselves." It could be any business alive.

✓ Do this

"To make sure no first-time mom feels like she has to choose between her health and her family."

The trap: reaching for a poster-worthy line. And if there's no personal reason underneath it, don't manufacture one. Not every business starts from a scar, and that's a pass.

2

Core ValuesWhat a client can count on you to believe

The beliefs a client can count on you to hold. What someone buying from you would feel, and what they'd say about you to a friend.

Why it matters. Values are the tiebreaker when a decision is close. They're also the raw material your Brand Voice is built from.

Read this first: "values" means two things

Internal operating valuesHow you and your team work when no client is watching, like "no excuses" or "ship it before it's perfect." Real, but not brand values. are how your team works. Brand values are what a client can count on you to believe. Only brand values go here.

If you want your internal values written down, the Builder offers at the end to add them as a marked appendix. No builder reads it.

How to find yours

Pick 3 to 5. For each, give the belief in a few words and what a client would actually experience because of it. These are the territories brand values usually live in:

TerritoryWhat it sounds like
Truth-telling"We'll tell you your offer is broken, even when you'd rather hear it's the traffic."
Standards of care"Nobody waits more than a day for an answer, even if the answer is 'not yet'."
Independence"You should be able to run this without us. That's the point."
The long game"We'll talk you out of the wrong thing even when you're ready to pay for it."
Who you turn away"We say no to people who won't do the work. It's kinder to say it early."
Results over activity"We report what happened, not what we did."
Simplicity"If it takes a diagram to explain, we built it wrong."
Practising it yourself"We don't do the things we tell you not to do."
✕ Not that

"Integrity. Excellence. Passion." Three words nobody could disagree with, guiding no decision.

✓ Do this

"Independence: you leave able to run this yourself. We'd rather lose the retainer than keep you dependent on us."

The trap: a bare list. A value with no meaning attached can't settle an argument. Then hold your values against your mission: a mission about rest under values about hustle should be a decision, not an accident.

3

GoalsWhere you're headed

Two horizons: what this business looks like in twelve months, and the bigger three-to-five-year picture. This is the shape of the business, not its revenue.

Why it matters. The twelve-month picture decides what you build next. The longer view tells you to say no when a well-paid opportunity walks you away from it.

How to find yours

Describe the year, not the number. How do you spend a week? Who's doing the work you do now? What have you stopped doing?

Then the bigger one, and let the human version in: freedom, impact, a team, a certain life.

✕ Not that

"Grow the business." Grow into what? You'd never know if you got there.

✓ Do this

"First coach hired and running the group calls without me. Events monthly instead of quarterly. I'm out of onboarding entirely."

The trap: answering with a revenue figure and stopping. Your targets belong in Products & Pricing. Here, describe what the number buys you.

4

Location & Service AreaWhere you are, and who you can serve

Two facts: where you're based, and where your clients are allowed to be.

Why it matters. Your location sets your hours, your currency, your tax position and which time zones your events can run in. Your service area is the difference between a marketing choice and a hard limit.

How to find yours

City and country for the base. Then your honest reach: global, a named set of countries, one country, or a region and a travel radius.

Then the part most people skip: what limits you. Licensing, regulation, insurance, tax, language, or time zones you won't work across. If there's nothing, write "no restrictions".

✕ Not that

"Anywhere, we're online!" Said without checking whether that's true.

✓ Do this

"Melbourne, Australia. Anywhere English-speaking: US, UK, Canada, Australia. No restrictions."

The trap: confusing this with your target market. This is about where you're able and allowed to deliver. An unwritten limit usually bites right after someone's paid you.

5

PositioningThe lane you own

Two halves: the lane you're in, and the stance you take in it. The lane is the shelf a customer would find you on. The stance is what you believe that others on that shelf don't.

Why it matters. The lane makes you findable. The stance makes you choosable. Without it, the only thing left to compete on is price.

How to find yours

For the lane, use the words a customer would use. Plain and narrow beats clever and broad.

For the stance, finish this: "Most people in my space think ___, and I think ___." Then test it: could a competitor read it and say "no, that's wrong"?

✕ Not that

"What makes us different is we really care about our clients." Nobody is arguing the other side.

✓ Do this

"Most people here think you need more leads. We think most businesses have enough leads and a broken offer."

The trap: "I care more." Every competitor says it. And hold competitor comparison for your Competitive Landscape: your stance should be true whether or not a single competitor exists.

6

PersonalityHow your brand behaves

If your brand walked into a room, how would it act? One direction, plus three adjectives.

Why it matters. Your Visual Style Guide and your Brand Voice both start here. This is the single answer that most shapes everything visible about your business.

How to find yours

Start from a direction: the Mentor, the Challenger, the Friend, the Expert, the Motivator, the Rebel or the Creator. Pick one, blend two, or name your own.

Then three adjectives for how it sounds and feels. The best sets usually contain one word that's slightly uncomfortable to admit.

✕ Not that

"Bold, innovative, professional, friendly, disruptive, caring, premium." A brand that's everything is nothing.

✓ Do this

"Mostly Mentor with a bit of Challenger. Warm, blunt, unhurried."

The trap: the aspirational personality. Picking "bold and provocative" when you're warm and steady is a costume you can't keep on.

Two founders, two personalities?

Don't average them. Look for the overlap first, then check whether the traits that look like a clash actually complement each other.

From there you have three honest options: build on the shared core, make the brand its own character, or run a deliberate duality with two named voices. The Builder lays them out and you choose.

7

Brand PromiseWhat they can always count on

Your standard. The experience every client gets, every time. The thing you'd never let slide.

Why it matters. It's the promise you can keep on your worst week, and it's what your reputation is built out of.

How to find yours

Finish this: "Every client who works with me (or with us) can always count on ___." Then test it: is this something I control? Responsiveness, honesty, depth and care, yes. Results, no.

✕ Not that

"You'll make six figures." An outcome you can't guarantee, so a promise you'll eventually break.

✓ Do this

"An answer within one working day, even if the answer is 'I don't know yet'."

The trap: promising a result. Results vary with the client, the market and the year. A standard doesn't. Save the outcome for your $Million Promise™.

06

Before and After

Here's the difference between a foundation that says nothing and one that could only belong to one business.

The vague version

"I'm a health coach who helps people live healthier, happier lives. My mission is to empower everyone to reach their full potential. I value integrity, excellence and passion. My goal is to grow the business."

After the seven elements
  • Mission: no first-time mom should have to choose between her health and her family
  • Values: honesty over hype, small wins over overhauls, no judgement ever
  • Goals: a second coach running the group calls in 12 months
  • Service area: UK and Ireland only, where her accreditation applies
  • Stance: everything happens with the baby in the room
  • Personality: the Friend: warm, real, a little funny
  • Brand promise: every plan fits a real mom's day, and you never wait more than a day to hear back

Now a designer knows what it looks like, a copywriter knows how it sounds, and a new mom knows it's for her.

Figure 06 · Same coach, same business. An illustrative example: it shows the level of detail, not a template to copy.

The first version could sit on ten thousand websites. The second could only belong to one coach.

07

The Hard Rules

These keep your foundation true and specific enough to build on. The Builder holds you to every one.

  1. Plain over polished. Say it the way a real person would. If it needs decoding, it's not done.
  2. Specific over safe. If it could belong to a thousand other businesses, it's not yours yet.
  3. True over aspirational. Build on how your business is, not how you wish it looked.
  4. Stay at brand altitude. No offers, no prices, no avatar, no competitors.
  5. Client-facing values. 3–5, each with what a client would experience. Internal values go only in the marked appendix.
  6. A stance, not a preference. Something a competitor would argue with.
  7. Brand promise, not outcome. Promise the standard you control.
  8. An explicit answer on limits. "None" if there are none. A blank is not an answer.
  9. A picture of the year. Your 12-month goal describes the business, not just the revenue.
  10. Nothing invented. The Builder won't make up your mission, values or numbers. If something's missing, it asks.

How It's Checked

This Foundation isn't scored out of 25. It's the truth about your business, written plainly, so it passes a Ready CheckA pass-or-fix check on each element. No points: every check has to be a clear yes before the profile is finished. instead.

The Builder also checks that nothing here belongs in another document, that "I" or "we" matches who runs the business, and that your values and your mission pull the same way.

If a check fails, it says so plainly and helps you sharpen it. It never writes the answer for you. And a detail you've marked as not decided yet counts as a pass.

Check Your Work

Almost every weak profile falls into one of these. Check yours against each one.

✕ Everything to everyone

A mission to "help people live better lives". It connects with no one.

✓ A specific change, for specific people

Narrow is what makes people feel seen.

✕ Internal values as brand values

"No excuses. Whatever it takes." A voice built on that sounds like pressure.

✓ Turned outward

Write what a client gets because you believe it. Keep the internal ones in the appendix.

✕ Wallpaper values

"Integrity. Excellence. Passion." A bare list guides no decision.

✓ Values with meaning

A one-line definition, and what a client would notice because of it.

✕ "I care more" as positioning

Every competitor says the same thing.

✓ A stance someone would argue with

If nobody could disagree, it isn't a stance.

✕ A result as your brand promise

"You'll make six figures." Results vary, so you'll eventually break it.

✓ A standard you control

How you show up, every time.

✕ The aspirational personality

"Bold and provocative" when you're warm and steady.

✓ How you really show up

With one adjective that's slightly uncomfortable to admit.

✕ A revenue figure as a goal

"$100K a month" and nothing else.

✓ What the number buys you

A team, different clients, time back. The number lives in Products & Pricing.

✕ The unwritten limit

"We work with anyone, we're online," with an accreditation that covers one country.

✓ Limits written down

Or "no restrictions" if there genuinely aren't any.

Is it ready?

  • Your mission is a real, specific change, not a generic poster.
  • Your purpose says why it's you chasing it. Genuinely absent is a pass. Vague isn't.
  • You have 3–5 client-facing values, each with what a client would experience.
  • Any internal operating values sit in the marked appendix, and nowhere in Core Values.
  • Your 12-month goal describes the business, and you've named the bigger vision.
  • You've given a base, a service area and an explicit answer on limits, including "none".
  • Your positioning has a clear lane and a stance a competitor would argue with.
  • Your personality is one direction plus three true adjectives.
  • Your brand promise is a standard you can always keep, separate from your $Million Promise™.
  • Nothing here could belong to a thousand other businesses.
  • Nothing here belongs in Products & Pricing or Competitive Landscape.
