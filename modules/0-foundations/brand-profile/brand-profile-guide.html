What Is the Business Brand Profile?

Your Business Brand Profile is the identity layer your whole business stands on — written down in one place.

It's a short, clear document that answers the questions everything else depends on.

Why does this business exist? What do you stand for? Where are you headed, and where in the world can you actually serve people?

It holds seven things: your mission and purpose, your core values, your goals, your location and service area, your positioning, your personality, and your brand promise.

Notice what's not on that list.

It doesn't ask what you sell. It doesn't ask what you charge. It doesn't ask who your avatar is or who your competitors are.

That's deliberate, and it's the thing most people get wrong about a brand document. Those are all real questions — they just belong to other tools, and answering them four times across four documents is how people give up somewhere in the third one.

This one stays high. It's the overarching truth about the business, not the operating detail.

It matters because it's the ground every other decision is built on.

Your look comes from your personality and your positioning. Your voice comes from your values and your personality. Your $Million Promise™ is built on the positioning you set here.

When the foundation is clear, all of that gets easier and more consistent.

When it's fuzzy, you feel it everywhere — in ads that don't sound like you, posts that wander, and offers that don't fit the person you actually serve.

This isn't a branding exercise about logos and colors — that comes next, in your Visual Style Guide.

This is about getting honest and specific about the business underneath all of it.

What the Business Brand Profile is NOT

It helps to be clear about what this isn't, because it's easy to confuse a few things.

It's not your $Million Promise™. That's the sharp marketing hook you build in Step 1 of the Formula. Your Profile holds your brand promise — the experience standard every client can count on. Different jobs: one is what you say first, one is who you are.

It's not your Visual Style Guide or your Brand Voice — two of the foundations you build next, and they both grow out of this one.

It's not where your offer, your pricing or your delivery live. That's your Products & Pricing doc.

It's not where your market or your competitors live. That's your Competitive Landscape doc.

It's not a mission statement you frame and forget. It's a working document you actually use to make decisions.

And it's not a fantasy version of your business. It's the true one, said plainly — because everything downstream inherits whatever you write here.

Where It Fits

The Business Brand Profile is Foundation 1 of 5 — the base layer of your brand, built before you start The $100K Day Formula™.

Think of it like building a house. The Formula is the house. But before you frame a single wall, you decide what house you're building and who it's for. That's the foundation.

There are five Brand Foundations, and they're built in order.

The Profile comes first because your look and your voice both grow out of it — you can't choose a color palette or a tone until you know your personality and what you stand for.

Each foundation owns its own questions

This is why the Profile refuses to ask about your offer or your market.

Five documents only work if each one owns a question and the others leave it alone. The moment two of them ask the same thing, you're doing the work twice — and worse, you end up with two slightly different answers and no way to tell which one is true.

So if the Builder ever declines to capture something you offer it, that's not a gap. It's the seam doing its job, and it'll tell you which document to put it in.

⚠ What breaks if this is weak

A fuzzy foundation doesn't stay quiet — it leaks into everything.

Your visual choices get random, because there's no personality guiding them.

Your voice drifts, because there are no values or traits to anchor it.

Your $Million Promise™ comes out generic, because the positioning underneath it was never pinned down.

That's why we set the foundation first — then work through the rest of the Brand Foundations, and only then start the Formula.

How You'll Build It

You don't write a brand profile by staring at a blank page waiting for wisdom.

You answer plain questions, one at a time, and the builder assembles your answers into one clean document.

Seven elements, thirteen questions. They come one per turn — never stacked, never three at once — and each one arrives with two things attached: why I'm asking, and an example so you can see the shape of a good answer.

That's on purpose. A question you don't understand the point of gets a worse answer, and a question with no example gets a guess.

It takes about twenty minutes.

The rest of this guide teaches each element in full — what it is, why it matters, how to find yours, and the trap that quietly weakens it.

What you believe + where you're going + how you show up = the ground everything else stands on.
"I don't know yet" is a real answer

Some of these you'll answer in a sentence. Others will make you stop and think, and one or two might make you realise you've never actually decided.

That's normal, and it's not a reason to invent something. A made-up answer is worse than a blank one, because everything downstream will be built on it.

Say what's true, mark what isn't settled yet, and come back to it once the tool that decides it has been built.

The Seven, In Full

1

Mission & Purpose — why this exists

Your mission is the reason the business exists beyond making money — the change you're trying to make.

Your purpose is the other half: why it's you chasing it.

Why it matters. Mission is what keeps you consistent when things get hard, and what makes the right people trust you. Purpose is what makes them believe you'll stay in the fight — it's the part a competitor can't copy. They can take your method. They can't take your reason.

How to find yours

For the mission, name the specific change you care about, for your specific people. What problem gets under your skin? What would still matter to you even if the money was handled?

For the purpose, go back to your own story. What did you live through that put you on this? Short is fine — one honest sentence beats a paragraph of backstory.

✓ Do this

"To make sure no first-time mom feels like she has to choose between her health and her family."

✕ Not that

"To empower people to become the best version of themselves." — could be any business alive.

The trap: reaching for a poster-worthy line. A mission that could belong to anyone belongs to no one. Get specific about your change and your people.

And if there's no personal reason underneath it, don't manufacture one. Not every business starts from a scar, and a fabricated origin story is the easiest thing in the world to see through.

2

Core Values — what a client can count on you to believe

Your core values are the beliefs a client can count on you to hold. What someone buying from you would feel, and what they'd say about you to a friend.

⚠ Read this before you answer — "values" means two different things

Internal operating values are how your team works when no client is watching. Your pace, your standards, how you make decisions. Things like "no excuses" or "ship it before it's perfect." Those are real and they matter.

They're not what this document holds.

Brand values are what a client can count on you to believe. That's what goes here — because your Brand Voice and your marketing get built straight out of it.

Plenty of businesses have a value that's both. That's a good sign. Just write the outward-facing version of it.

Get this backwards and the damage shows up two documents later: a Brand Voice built out of "no excuses" and "hustle" will sound like the thing your buyers are running from.

Why it matters. Values are the tiebreaker. When a decision is close — take the client or turn them down, refund or hold the line — these are what decide it. They're also the raw material your voice is built from.

How to find yours

Pick 3 to 5. For each one, give the belief in a few words and what a client would actually experience because of it.

If you're not sure where to start, these are the territories brand values usually live in — recognise the ones that are true for you, then say them in your own words:

✕ Not that

The trap: a bare list. A value with no meaning attached can't settle an argument, and settling arguments is the whole job.

One more check worth doing yourself: hold your values against your mission. If your mission is about rest and your values are all relentless hustle, a client will feel one thing in your marketing and something else in the room. That's not always wrong — but it should be a decision, not an accident.

3

Goals — where you're headed

Two horizons: what this business looks like in twelve months, and the bigger three-to-five-year picture.

Note the word looks. This is the shape of the business, not its revenue.

Why it matters. The twelve-month picture decides what you build next. A business heading for a small team builds differently from one heading for a solo practice — same revenue, completely different decisions. The longer view keeps you pointed: when an opportunity shows up that pays well but walks you away from it, this is what tells you to say no.

How to find yours

Describe the year, not the number. What's genuinely different about how you spend a week? Who's doing the work you're doing now? What have you stopped doing?

Then the bigger one — and let the human version in. Freedom, impact, a team, a certain life.

✓ Do this

"First coach hired and running the group calls without me. Events monthly instead of quarterly. I'm out of onboarding entirely."

✕ Not that

"Grow the business." — grow into what? You'd never know if you got there.

The trap: answering with a revenue figure and stopping. Your targets belong in your Products & Pricing doc, and putting them here means you'll write them twice and never describe the business at all. Keep the number — put it where it lives, and here describe what the number buys you.

And if you genuinely don't know what the delivery looks like yet, say so. That's a Money Model question, and pretending to an org chart you don't believe in makes this document worse, not better.

4

Location & Service Area — where you are, and who you can serve

Two facts that shape more than people expect: where you're based, and where your clients are allowed to be.

Why it matters. Your location sets your working hours, your currency, your tax position, and which time zones your events can realistically run in. Your service area is the difference between a marketing decision and a hard limit — and everything downstream depends on which one it is.

How to find yours

City and country for the base. Then the honest answer on reach — global, a named set of countries, one country, or a region and a travel radius.

Then the part most people skip: what limits you. Licensing, regulation, insurance, tax, language, or time zones you won't work across. If there's nothing, write "no restrictions" — an explicit none is an answer, a blank isn't.

✓ Do this

"Melbourne, Australia. Anywhere English-speaking — US, UK, Canada, Australia. No restrictions."

✕ Not that

"Anywhere, we're online!" — said without checking whether that's actually true.

The trap: confusing this with your target market. Where you'd like to sell is a different question, and your Competitive Landscape and your Golden Avatar™ handle it. This one is about what's possible: where are you able and allowed to deliver?

An unwritten limit is the one that bites, and it usually bites right after someone's paid you. Written down, it becomes a filter on the front of your funnel instead of a refund at the back of it.

5

Positioning — the lane you own

Two halves: the lane you're in, and the stance you take in it.

The lane is the shelf a customer would find you on. The stance is what you believe that others on that shelf don't.

Why it matters. The lane makes you findable — people can only buy what they can categorise. The stance makes you choosable. Without it you're a slightly different version of the person standing next to you, and the only thing left to compete on is price.

How to find yours

For the lane, use the words a customer would use, not the words you'd use in a pitch. Plain and narrow beats clever and broad.

For the stance, finish this: "Most people in my space think ___, and I think ___."

Then test it with one question: could a competitor read this and say "no, that's wrong"? If nobody could disagree with it, it isn't a stance — it's a platitude.

✓ Do this

"Most people here think you need more leads. We think most businesses already have enough leads and a broken offer — more traffic just makes the leak faster."

✕ Not that

"What makes us different is we really care about our clients." — nobody is arguing the other side.

The trap: "I care more." Every competitor says it, so it sets you apart from none of them.

The second trap is sliding into competitor comparison — naming rivals, pricing against them. Hold that for your Competitive Landscape, which is built for it. Your stance should be true whether or not a single competitor exists.

6

Personality — how your brand behaves

If your brand walked into a room, how would it act? One direction, plus three adjectives.

Why it matters. Your Visual Style Guide and your Brand Voice both start here. A Challenger brand and a Friend brand get different colors, different fonts, and a completely different way of writing an email. This is the single answer that most shapes everything visible about your business.

How to find yours

Start from a direction: the Mentor · the Challenger · the Friend · the Expert · the Motivator · the Rebel · the Creator. Pick one, blend two, or name your own.

Then three adjectives for how it sounds and feels. Bold? Warm? Playful? Precise? Calm? Blunt?

The direction gives you the character. The three adjectives make it usable — they're what a designer and a copywriter actually work from when you're not in the room.

✓ Do this

"Mostly Mentor with a bit of Challenger. Warm, blunt, unhurried."

✕ Not that

"Bold, innovative, professional, friendly, disruptive, caring, premium." — a brand that's everything is a brand that's nothing.

The trap: the aspirational personality. Picking "bold and provocative" when you're actually warm and steady means wearing a costume you can't keep on. The real version is easier to sustain and more magnetic anyway.

A useful tell: the best sets of three usually contain one word that's slightly uncomfortable. If all three are flattering, they're probably aspirational. "Warm, blunt, unhurried" works because "blunt" costs something to admit.

7

Brand Promise — what they can always count on

Your standard. The experience every single client gets, every time — the thing you'd never let slide.

Why it matters. This is the promise you can keep on your worst week, when you're behind and tired and the client is being difficult. It's what your reputation actually gets built out of. And it's the one thing in this document your team has to be able to deliver without you in the room.

How to find yours

Finish this: "Every client who works with us can always count on ___."

Then check it against one test: is this something I control? Responsiveness, honesty, depth, clarity, care — you control those. Results, you don't.

✓ Do this

"An answer within one working day, even if the answer is 'I don't know yet'."

✕ Not that

"You'll make six figures." — an outcome you can't guarantee, so it's a promise you'll eventually break.

The trap: promising a result. Results vary with the client, the market and the year. A standard doesn't.

Keep this separate from your $Million Promise™, too. That one is the sharp hook that stops a stranger scrolling. This one is the standard behind how you deliver. One is what you say first; the other is who you are.

A Worked Example

Here's the difference between a foundation that says nothing and one that could only belong to one business.

The vague version

"I'm a health coach who helps people live healthier, happier lives through personalized wellness plans. My mission is to empower everyone to reach their full potential. I value integrity, excellence, and passion. My goal is to grow the business."

It sounds fine, and it says nothing. You could paste it onto ten thousand other coaches.

Now the same coach, sharpened.

Mission: "No first-time mom should have to choose between her health and her family."

Purpose: "I did it the hard way after my first — lost fourteen months to exhaustion I didn't have to lose."

Core values: Honesty over hype — I'll tell you the plan isn't working in week one, not week ten. Small wins over overhauls — you'll never get a plan that assumes you have an hour. No judgement, ever — you can tell me you ate the cake.

Goals: 12 months — a second coach running the group calls, and I'm out of the daily check-ins. 3–5 years — a programme other coaches deliver, so it works when I'm not in the room.

Location & service area: Leeds, UK. UK and Ireland only — my nutrition accreditation doesn't carry outside them.

Positioning: Lane — health coaching for new moms. Stance — most coaches think you need to carve out time away from the baby. I think that advice is why moms quit, and everything I build happens with the baby in the room.

Personality: The Friend — warm, real, a little funny.

Brand promise: "Every plan fits a real mom's day, and you'll never wait more than a day to hear back."

Same coach. But now a designer knows what it looks like, a copywriter knows how it sounds, a new mom knows it's for her — and nobody has to guess whether she can legally take a client in Ohio.

The Hard Rules

These keep your foundation true and specific enough to build on, and the Builder holds you to them.

Plain over polished. Every element should be said the way a real person would say it. If it needs decoding, it's not done.

Specific over safe. If an answer could belong to a thousand other businesses, it's not yours yet. Push for the version only you could write.

True over aspirational. Build on how your business actually is, not how you wish it looked. The real version is more magnetic and easier to keep.

Stay at brand altitude. No offers, no prices, no avatar, no competitors. If it belongs to another document, it goes there — even when it feels useful to have it all in one place.

Client-facing values. 3–5, each with what a client would experience. Internal operating values are a different thing and they don't live here.

A stance, not a preference. Positioning needs something a competitor would argue with. "I care more" isn't one.

Brand promise, not outcome. Promise the standard you control, not a result that varies.

An explicit answer on limits. Your service area needs a stated "none" if there are no restrictions. A blank is not an answer.

A picture on the year. Your 12-month goal describes the business, not just the revenue. The revenue lives in Products & Pricing.

Nothing invented. The Builder will not make up your mission, your values or your numbers, and it will not offer you an example containing a fact about a business. If something's missing, it asks.

Common Mistakes & Fixes

Almost every weak profile falls into one of these.

1. The everything-to-everyone foundation

What it looks like: a mission to "help people live better lives" and a stance of "we just do great work."

Why it happens: Narrowing feels like turning away business, so you keep it wide.

Why it costs you: A brand for everyone connects with no one, and every layer above it inherits the fog.

The fix: pick the specific change and the specific people. Narrow is what makes people feel seen.

2. Answering with your internal values

What it looks like: "No excuses. Jump out the plane and build the parachute on the way down. Whatever it takes."

Why it happens: The question sounds like it's asking how you run your business — and those values are usually the ones you feel most strongly about, so they come out first.

Why it costs you: Your Brand Voice is built from these. Internal drive values produce a client-facing voice that sounds like pressure, which is the opposite of what most buyers are looking for.

The fix: keep them — they're real — but turn them outward. Ask what a client gets because you believe that, and write that instead.

3. Wallpaper values

What it looks like: "Integrity. Excellence. Passion." — a bare list.

Why it happens: Those words feel safe and expected, so they're the first ones you reach for.

Why it costs you: Values with no meaning attached don't guide any decision or signal any difference.

The fix: give each value a one-line definition, and say what a client would actually notice because of it.

4. "I care more" as positioning

What it looks like: "What makes me different is I really care about my clients."

Why it happens: It's true and it feels meaningful, so it seems like enough.

Why it costs you: Every competitor says the exact same thing, so it doesn't set you apart at all.

The fix: find the belief a competitor would argue with. If nobody could disagree, it's not a stance.

5. Confusing brand promise with a result

What it looks like: a brand promise of "you'll make six figures."

Why it happens: The big outcome feels like the strongest thing to promise.

Why it costs you: Results vary, so a result-as-promise is one you'll eventually break — and a broken promise costs trust.

The fix: promise the standard you always control — how you show up — and save the outcome for your $Million Promise™.

6. The aspirational personality

What it looks like: picking "bold and provocative" when you're actually warm and steady.

Why it happens: A louder personality seems like it'll get more attention.

Why it costs you: You can't keep up a costume, so your look and voice end up inconsistent and off.

The fix: build on how you genuinely show up. Aim for one adjective that's slightly uncomfortable to admit — that's the sign it's true.

7. A revenue figure instead of a goal

What it looks like: a 12-month goal of "$100K a month" and nothing else.

Why it happens: The number is the thing you think about most, so it's the answer that comes out.

Why it costs you: The number belongs in Products & Pricing, and if it's all that's here then nothing in this document describes what the business becomes — which is what your next decisions actually depend on.

The fix: keep the number where it lives, and here describe what it buys you. A team? Different clients? Time back? What does the business look like at that size?

8. The unwritten limit

What it looks like: "we work with anyone, we're online" — from a business whose accreditation only covers one country.

Why it happens: Nobody thinks about licensing or insurance until it's a problem, and "anywhere" feels like the ambitious answer.

Why it costs you: The limit surfaces at the worst possible moment — usually after someone has already paid you.

The fix: write the limits down, or write "no restrictions" if there genuinely aren't any. Then it's a filter on the front of your funnel instead of a refund at the back.

Is It Ready?

This foundation isn't scored out of 25 — it's the truth about your business, written plainly. So it passes a Ready Check instead: every item a clear yes.

  • Your mission is a real, specific change — not a generic poster.
  • Your purpose says why it's you chasing it. (Genuinely absent is a pass. Vague isn't.)
  • You have 3–5 client-facing values, each with what a client would experience.
  • Your 12-month goal describes the business, not just a revenue figure — and you've named the bigger vision.
  • You've given a base, a service area, and an explicit answer on limits (including "none").
  • Your positioning has a clear lane AND a stance a competitor would argue with.
  • Your personality is one direction plus 3 true adjectives.
  • Your brand promise is a standard you can always keep — separate from your $Million Promise™.
  • The specificity test: nothing here could belong to a thousand other businesses.
  • The altitude test: nothing here belongs in Products & Pricing or Competitive Landscape.

All boxes checked? Your Business Brand Profile is locked — and it's the ground your Visual Style Guide, your Brand Voice, and your whole Formula stand on.
