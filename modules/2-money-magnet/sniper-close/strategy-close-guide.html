What The Strategy Close Is — and What It Isn't

The Strategy Close is one of three ways your Sniper Presentation™ can end. Instead of asking the room to buy, you ask them to book a session with you.

That session is called a PASS™ — a Paid Audit Strategy Session. It's where the real selling happens.

Here's the shift that makes this work. You are not booking a sales call. You are booking a diagnosis.

Your client sits down with you for an hour. You walk them through their own business, step by step, against your Magic Formula™. You score every step. You show them exactly where they're strong, exactly where they're broken, and exactly what to do about it.

By the end they have a written plan. Then you ask one question: would you like my help?

What it is NOT:

It's not a discovery call with a fancy name. A discovery call is you asking questions to find out if they can afford you. A PASS™ is you delivering a professional assessment they'd have paid for on its own.

It's not a free consultation you started charging for. If you'd have run the same call for free, you haven't earned the fee. The deliverable is what earns it — the audit, the plan, the report.

It's not a pitch with an audit bolted on the front. You are genuinely diagnosing. If the honest answer is that they don't need you, that's the answer.

The Ask — Closing From the Stage

Your Sniper Presentation™ runs its course. You've made the offer, walked the value stack, anchored, and revealed the price. Now comes the ask.

With the Strategy Close, the ask is not "buy this." It's "book a session with me."

The four beats of the ask:

Beat 1

Name the session

Don't say "book a call." Nobody wants a call. Give the session a real name that says what they get.

Use this shape: [Timeline] + [Outcome] + [Session Type]. So: "90-Day Client Flow Mapping Session." Or "The Studio Owner Growth Audit."

A named session sounds like a deliverable. "A call" sounds like a sales pitch, because it usually is one.

Beat 2

State the fee — and where it goes

Say the number plainly. It comes from your Money Model, and it's yours to set.

Then remove the sting immediately: the fee comes off your first payment if you decide to work together. Nobody pays twice.

Beat 3

Make the promise that removes the risk

This is the beat most people miss, and it's the one that makes a paid session make sense.

They leave with a written plan whether they hire you or not.

Say it out loud. That single promise is the difference between "you want me to pay for a sales call?" and "that's a cheap way to get an expert to look at my business."

Beat 4

Give the steps, then stop talking

Numbered. On screen. Brain-dead simple. Click the link, pick a time, pay, done.

Then stop. Don't fill the silence, don't re-explain, don't apologise for the fee. The pause is doing work.

How it sounds

"Here's what I'd like to do. I run a 90-Day Client Flow Mapping Session — it's [your fee], it takes about an hour, and we go through your business step by step against the exact system I just showed you."

"You'll leave with a scored assessment and a written plan. That's yours whether you work with me or not."

"And if you do decide to work together, the [fee] comes straight off your first payment. So you're never paying twice."

"Link's on the screen. Pick a time that works. I'll see you there."

Free or Paid — and When to Switch

The Strategy Close runs two ways: the session is free, or the session is paid. Here's the honest guidance.

Paid is the goal. A fee filters. People who pay show up, arrive ready, and take the hour seriously. People who don't pay treat it as optional — and a diary full of no-shows will make you hate this close.

Free is training wheels. If you've never run one of these, run a few free. Not because free is better, but because you need reps before you can charge with a straight face.

What actually changes when it's free:

Nothing about the session. Same questions, same audit, same scoring, same plan, same report, same close. If you run a weaker session because it's free, you've learned the wrong lesson and you'll never be ready to charge.

What changes is who turns up. Free sessions run at around a 50% show rate. Half your calendar evaporates. That's the cost of no filter, and it's exactly why you move to paid as soon as you can.

When to start charging:

When you've run enough sessions that you're not nervous, and enough of them have converted that you believe the session is worth the money. You'll know. The tell is that you stop hoping they say yes and start genuinely not minding either way.

It's still a PASS™ when it's free. You're just not charging yet.

The PASS™ — What Actually Happens in the Session

A PASS™ is about an hour. It has five beats and they run in order.

The whole thing rests on one idea: you're not selling coaching, you're delivering a diagnosis. Enrolment is just what happens when the diagnosis is thorough and the expert is obviously credible.

Beat 1 · about 5 minutes

The Goal

Three questions, in this order.

Where do you want to be in 90 days — what's the specific result? Where are you right now on a scale of 1 to 10? What are the top two or three things getting in the way?

Write down their exact words. You'll use them at the end.

Beat 2 · 15–20 minutes

The Audit

Nine steps. One question each. Score every step out of 5. Total out of 45.

Capture only. No coaching. No solving. This is the hardest discipline in the whole session and it's non-negotiable.

They'll say something broken and you'll want to fix it on the spot. Don't. Say "got it," write it down, move to the next step.

Every fix you hand over here is a reason not to hire you.

Beat 3 · 3–5 minutes

The Gap

Now you talk. Give them the total, name how many areas are solid and how many need attention, and point at the phase where the gaps cluster.

Then tie it back to what they told you in Beat 1 — "which lines up exactly with what you said about not getting consistent clients."

That's the moment the audit stops being a quiz and starts being a mirror.

Finish on hope, not doom: these are specific, and they're fixable. Here's what I'd do.

Beat 4 · 25–35 minutes

The Plan

This is the part they paid for. For every step scoring 3 or below, you give three things:

Root cause — one sentence on why the gap exists. Recommendation — what the fixed version looks like. Homework — one specific action they can start on their own.

Steps scoring 4 or 5 get a sentence: that's solid, keep doing it.

Here's the discipline: direction, not delivery. Tell them where to go. Don't walk them there. The homework should be real, doable, and deliberately incomplete.

Beat 5 · 3–5 minutes

The Close

Two questions and an ask. Don't improvise this part.

Question one: based on everything we've mapped out — how confident are you that this plan gets you to [their goal] if it's done properly?

They'll say high. Of course they will. You just spent forty minutes proving it.

Question two: and how confident are you that you can do all of this on your own?

They'll say low. Because it's a lot, and they know it.

The ask: "Would you like my help?"

Then stop talking. Let the silence sit. Do not fill it, do not soften it, do not start listing bonuses. Silence reads as confidence, and you've earned it.

If they say yes:

Move straight into onboarding like it was always going to happen. "Great — I'll put the [fee] you paid today toward your first payment, so you're not paying twice. Want to get started?"

If they say "let me think about it":

Take it gracefully. Tell them you'll send the full report today, and follow up in a day or two. Then actually do it.

Don't discount. Don't push. The report is your follow-up — it keeps the conversation alive without you having to chase.

The PASS™ Instrument — Why It's Your Magic Formula™

You can't audit someone against nothing. You need an instrument — a fixed set of things you check, in the same order, every time.

Yours already exists. It's your Magic Formula™ — three phases, nine steps.

That's the whole trick, and it's why nobody can copy this session. Anyone can run a generic business audit. Only you can audit someone against your nine-step system, because only you have it.

Turning nine steps into nine diagnostics:

Your Magic Formula™ gives each step a name and an outcome. To audit with it, each step needs three more things.

What you addWhat it means
Step CurrencyThe measure that tells you where they stand on this step. A number, a rate, or a confidence level.
QuestionsOne to three things you'd actually say out loud to get that measure.
AnchorsWhat a 5 looks like. What a 1 looks like. The middle takes care of itself.

Some measures are objective — a number you capture and score yourself. How many leads a week. What they charge. How many clients they've got.

Some are subjective — a confidence or clarity level they score themselves, 1 to 5, live. How clear are you on who your ideal client is? How consistent are you with follow-up?

Both are valid. What kills a session is a vague question. "How's your marketing going?" tells you nothing. "How many qualified leads did you get last week?" tells you everything.

All nine steps, calibrated once, become your PASS™ Instrument — built one time and used in every session you ever run.

Note: your Step Currency is the per-step measure. Don't confuse it with your Core Currency, which is the single outcome your $Million Promise™ delivers. Same idea, different scale.

The Report — Your Promise, Kept

You promised them a written plan whether they hire you or not. The report is you keeping it.

Send it within the hour. Every time. Enrolled or not.

What's in it:

Their 90-day goal, in their words. Their current reality and their stated obstacles. The full scorecard — all nine steps, scored. Every recommendation with its root cause and homework. And a short priority plan: the three things to do first.

Why it matters more than it looks:

For the person who enrolled, it's the first deliverable of your relationship, and it makes you look organised on day one.

For the person who didn't, it's a document with your thinking all over it sitting in their inbox. When they're ready — and some of them will be — you're who they come back to.

And for the person who paid and said no, it's the reason they don't feel robbed. That protects your reputation in a market where people talk.

Two say they'll think about it. Nobody buys. She's spent five hours and has nothing to show for it — and neither do they.

Four people book. All four show up — they paid, so they turn up.

In each session she runs her nine steps, scores them, and names the gaps. One scores 31 out of 45 with everything broken in her middle phase. The coach shows her exactly why her leads dry up, gives her three fixes and one piece of homework, then asks the two questions.

Confident the plan works? Very. Confident you can do it alone? Not at all.

The offer never changed. The ask did — and so did who was in the conversation.

Hard Rules

Name the session, never "a call." A call sounds like a pitch. A named session sounds like a deliverable.

The fee comes off the first payment. Say it in the ask and honour it in the close. Nobody pays twice.

They leave with a plan either way. This is the promise that makes a paid session reasonable. Break it once and the close stops working.

Capture only during the audit. No coaching, no solving, no fixing. Every gap you close for free is a reason not to hire you.

Direction, not delivery. Tell them where to go. Don't take them there. Homework should be real and deliberately incomplete.

Two questions, then the ask, then silence. Don't improvise the close and don't fill the pause.

Send the report within the hour. Every session. No exceptions.

Your numbers are yours. The session fee comes from your Money Model. Never quote someone else's price or someone else's conversion rate as if it were yours.

Common Mistakes That Kill the Close

Coaching during the audit

They describe a broken funnel and you can't help yourself — you fix it right there. It feels generous. It is generous. And it's why they don't hire you.

You've just proved they can get value from you for free. The audit is for finding gaps, not filling them.

The fix: say "got it," write it down, move on. Every single time. Save it all for Beat 4.

Running a sales call with an audit stapled to the front

You rush the nine steps to get to the pitch. The client feels it. The audit becomes an obvious formality and the whole thing curdles.

The fix: give the audit its full 15–20 minutes and mean it. If the honest diagnosis is that they don't need you, say so. You'll close more by being willing to lose some.

Apologising for the fee

"It's only a small amount, just to cover my time…" The second you justify it, you've told them it isn't worth it.

The fix: state the fee, state what they get, state that it comes off the first payment. Then move on like it's the most normal thing in the world — because it is.

Filling the silence after the ask

You ask "would you like my help?" and two seconds later you're listing bonuses. You've just answered your own question, and the answer you gave was "no."

The fix: ask it, then close your mouth. Count to ten in your head if you have to. Let them think.

Vague diagnostic questions

"How's your content going?" gets you "yeah, alright I think." Now you can't score it, and your report is mush.

The fix: calibrate every step before you run a single session. A real measure, a real question, and a clear picture of what a 5 and a 1 look like.

Not sending the report

They didn't buy, so you quietly skip it. You've now taken money — or an hour — and broken the one promise that justified the ask.

The fix: send it within the hour, no matter the outcome. The ones who didn't buy today are exactly who it's for.

Staying free too long

Free feels safer, so you never switch. Half your bookings no-show, your calendar fills with people who were never going to buy, and you start resenting the whole thing.

The fix: run a handful free to find your feet, then charge. The fee isn't a barrier — it's the filter that makes this close work.
